Steel / Sources and method

How we counted the steel

Start with every plant in the September 2026 tracker that has crude-steel capacity under construction. Follow its disclosed ownership and check how much the new equipment adds after older furnaces close.

Which furnaces count?

The September 2026 v1 Global Iron and Steel Tracker lists steel furnaces under construction at 107 plants in 31 countries. Its map gives a number for 106 plants and marks the capacity of Ch’ollima in North Korea as greater than zero. The 106 figures sum to 171.388 million metric tonnes of annual crude-steel capacity (Mt/yr). All shares of world capacity use that quantified total. Ch’ollima remains in the project ledger.

We used the September public map from the same tracker release. It supplies the GEM plant ID, coordinates and rated capacity grouped by status and process. We included basic oxygen, electric arc, induction, open-hearth and other steelmaking furnaces. Direct-reduced iron, blast furnaces and rolling mills represent other stages of production. Adding their capacities to a steelmaking furnace would count metal more than once.

We joined individual construction furnace records from the public API to the fixed map using their GEM plant IDs. The map sets the September denominator. The API snapshot is dated November 25, 2026. Its unit identifiers give readers a way to check which equipment GEM associates with each plant. The full form-gated September workbook was unavailable for this investigation.

The frozen API includes 160 steelmaking units. Grouping them by plant and furnace method gives one numerical difference from the release. At BAP Al-Khair, the API gives 1.5 Mt/yr and the September file gives 1.7. The map keeps the September figure. The resulting worldwide totals are 171.188 and 171.388 Mt/yr respectively. Unit reconciliation and source checksums

Announced furnaces are a separate status. Their disclosed capacity totals 659.823 Mt/yr worldwide, including 315.506 Mt/yr in India. Adding those ratings would put India ahead of China in the combined pipeline. All three dashboard measures exclude announced furnaces. A plant can contain one furnace under construction and another still on paper.

How tonnes move from a plant to its owners

For each plant we matched GEM’s parent entity IDs to company names and headquarters countries, tracing local subsidiaries through the available ownership records. When an issuer disclosed its effective stake in the steelmaking business, that filing took precedence. We assigned headquarters only to identified industrial companies, including source-reported owners named on the published industrial-owner list. Issuer disclosures support a subset. Named banks, investment vehicles, individuals, unions and opaque holding or trading companies stay outside that list. A headquarters country identifies where that company is based. It says nothing about the destination of its steel, the nationality of each shareholder or the source of each loan.

For a 60% stake in a 6 Mt/yr project, the owners’ map assigns 3.6 Mt/yr to that sponsor’s country. A sponsor is domestic when it shares the plant’s country and overseas when its headquarters are elsewhere. A whole mill can have domestic and overseas shareholders. The equity totals divide its tonnes among them. The dashboard also groups whole plants by the ownership we can identify.

Grey includes an undisclosed percentage, an unidentified owner or headquarters, and stakes held by financial investors with no identified industrial sponsor behind them. A named financial owner can therefore appear in the ledger with a disclosed stake and an unassigned industrial headquarters. At a partially resolved site we keep the known shares and put the balance in grey. We never split outstanding shares equally among owners or rescale a conflicting set of percentages to 100%.

At Asia United Steel in Kazakhstan, GEM lists the local project company as its own parent. The regional authority reports foreign financing without disclosing industrial shareholders or their stakes. We keep the company’s reported office in the ledger and put the project’s 1.2 Mt/yr in the unassigned column. Asia United Steel project record

What the replacement check changes

We started with the largest plant records. To count a positive addition, we looked for evidence of physical steelmaking construction at the September cutoff and a project rating for new crude-steel capacity. At existing works we used the increase over the retiring capacity. At sites linked by a formal capacity-transfer plan we deducted the old furnaces in the plan, even when they were elsewhere. Where owners publish ratings in US short tons, the ledger records the basis of any conversion to metric tonnes.

Zero means a project contributes no positive addition to the September construction count. Examples include a replacement smaller than its predecessor, work already finished and a project suspended before September. A blank net amount in the download means the evidence is inconclusive. The ledger records declines at replacement sites separately. Measuring the change in all world capacity would also require counting unrelated closures.

We made 107 site-specific checks covering 171.388 Mt/yr of the September list. Of 107 sites, 12 have a measured positive addition and 30 contribute zero. At 65 sites the net amount is unresolved. Each unresolved site has a project-level note explaining the missing evidence. The checked positives total 26.7 Mt/yr. Project evidence varies by country, so this subset cannot estimate worldwide ownership of net additions.

Holding every unresolved project at its full rating and applying all measured corrections gives 121.875 Mt/yr. Domestic owners account for 56.43 Mt/yr in that scenario. It is one way to test the effect of the documented corrections.
