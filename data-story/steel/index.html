Who owns the next steel boom?

China and India host nearly half the steel capacity under construction. Luxembourg ranks third by the capacity its companies own, all of it at mills abroad.

At Hazira in Gujarat, three new converters will give a steelworks on the Tapi estuary room to make another six million tonnes of crude steel a year. An Indian company, ArcelorMittal Nippon Steel India, runs the works. Its two steelmaking sponsors own it through a holding company in Luxembourg. ArcelorMittal, based there, holds 60%. Japan’s Nippon Steel holds 40%. 1

On a map of mills, all six million tonnes belong in Gujarat. On a map of owners, 3.6 million go to Luxembourg and 2.4 million to Japan. We followed the same trail for steel furnaces under construction around the world. Companies based in the country where a furnace is going up account for about half the capacity on the construction list. Once we account for replacements and projects already finished or halted, the share we can trace to local owners falls below half. That calculation still counts projects with uncertain gains at their full ratings.

Where the furnaces are going up

Global Energy Monitor’s September 2026 tracker records 171.4 million tonnes of annual crude-steel capacity under construction at 106 plants in 30 countries. A 107th plant, Ch’ollima in North Korea, has no published rating. China and India together account for 46.6% of the measured capacity. Iran ranks third, followed by Sweden and Mexico. 2

Where the owners are based

Companies based in the country where the steel is being built account for 87.7 million tonnes a year, or 51.2% of the construction total. That clears the halfway mark by just 2.0 million tonnes. Overseas industrial owners account for 25.5 million. For the other 58.2 million, the available ownership records stop short of an industrial sponsor, a percentage or a headquarters country. 2a

China and India lead on both sides of the map. Of the 42.3 million tonnes being built in China, 27.2 million trace to Chinese companies. In India, local companies account for 21.6 million of 37.6 million. Hazira and Anakapalle account for India’s identified overseas share. GEM includes both sites in its construction list. At Anakapalle, ArcelorMittal still described the 8.2 million tonne project as announced in its September-quarter report. Its rating counts in the construction figure, while the timing of the new capacity remains unconfirmed. 2b

Luxembourg ranks third among the headquarters countries we can identify. Its steel companies account for 12.5 million tonnes of annual furnace capacity under construction, all of it abroad. Japan’s 5.7 million tonnes are also entirely overseas. Neither country has a furnace on this construction list.

One of those Luxembourg companies is Ternium. Its rolling mill at Pesquería in Mexico turns slabs into coils. A new electric steel shop next door will let the site make 2.6 million tonnes of its own slabs a year. Ternium’s filings give it an effective 89.25% stake in its Mexican business, including shares held through Ternium Argentina. That puts 2.32 million tonnes in Luxembourg. The remaining 0.28 million stay unassigned. 3

Sometimes the shareholder is known and the map still shows grey. At Ras al-Khair in Saudi Arabia, Baosteel of China holds half the venture. Saudi Aramco holds a quarter. Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund holds the last quarter. We can name that investor, but its stake tells us nothing about a separate steelmaking sponsor. We leave its share in grey alongside the shares for which no owner or percentage was disclosed. 4

How much steel does a new furnace add?

At Port Talbot in South Wales, the ownership trail runs back to India. Tata Steel’s new electric furnace can make 3.2 million tonnes a year. The old works could make about five million. The project brings steelmaking back to Port Talbot, but adds zero to the site’s previous capacity. 5

The old furnace can even be in a different town. In Hubei, six furnaces at three plants are due to give up capacity for a 2.25 million tonne steelworks in Huangshi. At Luleå in Sweden the arithmetic goes the other way. SSAB rates the new mill at 2.5 million tonnes against the old mill’s 2.3 million. The gain is 200,000 tonnes a year. 6 7

Timing matters, too. In Bangladesh, a reporter for Prothom Alo visited in June and found Meghna’s steel plant already built and waiting for gas. Its 1.4 million tonnes remain on the September tracker list, but the construction was finished before the cutoff. 8

Apply the corrections we could measure and leave every unresolved project at its full furnace rating. Under that assumption, the construction total falls from 171.4 to 121.9 million tonnes a year. The share attributable to owners based in the host country falls from 51.2% to 46.3%. On this measure another 4.5 million of the 43.6 million unassigned tonnes would have to trace to domestic owners for their share to reach half. 9

We checked all 107 plants. At 12 in 6 countries we could document an increase. Together they add 26.7 million tonnes a year. Plants in India account for 17.1 million of that total. In Iran we could trace 1.4 million to new billet furnaces at Mianeh and Abarkouh. 10 11 At 30 sites we found zero eligible additions. At the remaining 65, the evidence leaves the net amount open.

Hazira’s six million tonnes count in every measure. Port Talbot’s 3.2 million appear on the construction list and drop out once its old works are counted. Both projects move tonnes across borders on the ownership map. Only one expands steelmaking capacity. That distinction, still unresolved at dozens of sites, keeps the global ownership of the gains an open question.
