Who owns the next steel boom?

China and India lead the world in steel capacity under construction. Where are the companies behind the mills based?

At Hazira in Gujarat, three new converters will give a steelworks on the Tapi estuary room to make another six million tonnes of crude steel a year. Its operator, ArcelorMittal Nippon Steel India, is Indian. Follow the shares through a Luxembourg holding company and the six million tonnes split two ways. ArcelorMittal, based in Luxembourg, owns 60%. Nippon Steel, based in Japan, owns 40%. 1

On a map of mills, all six million tonnes belong in Gujarat. On a map of industrial owners, 3.6 million trace to Luxembourg and 2.4 million to Japan. We made both maps for the world’s steel furnaces under construction. Hazira is a clear case of overseas ownership. Elsewhere a mill can have local and foreign owners, and sometimes the documents tell us only who owns part of it.

Where the furnaces are going up

Global Energy Monitor’s September 2026 tracker puts 171.4 million tonnes of annual crude-steel capacity under construction at 106 plants in 30 countries. One more plant, Ch’ollima in North Korea, has no published capacity figure. China accounts for 42.3 million tonnes and India for 37.6 million. Together they hold 46.6% of the measured total. Iran comes third at 15.8 million, ahead of Sweden and Mexico. 2

Where the owners are based

Companies based in the country where a furnace is being built account for 88.3 million tonnes a year, or 51.5% of the tracker’s measured construction capacity. Companies based overseas account for 26.5 million. The remaining 56.5 million tonnes combine stakes held by financial investors with shares whose industrial owner, percentage or headquarters we could not establish. Even if every unassigned tonne belonged to an overseas company, domestic owners would still account for a majority of the construction list. 2a

Of the 42.3 million tonnes being built in China, 27.2 million trace to owners based there. In India, local companies account for 21.6 million of 37.6 million. Two projects account for India’s entire 14.2 million tonnes of identified overseas ownership. One is Hazira. The other is Anakapalle, where the same two partners propose another 8.2 million tonnes. GEM counts it as under construction. ArcelorMittal still called it announced in its September-quarter report, so we leave it out of the checked additions. 2b

Among the headquarters we can locate, China, India and Luxembourg have the largest totals. Luxembourg illustrates what an ownership map can reveal. Companies based there account for 12.5 million tonnes of annual capacity in other countries. Luxembourg itself has no furnace on the tracker’s construction list.

In Mexico, the distinction runs through one supply chain. Ternium’s rolling mill at Pesquería turns slabs into coils. Those slabs have arrived from Brazil and from outside suppliers. The electric steel shop under construction next door will let the site make 2.6 million tonnes of slabs a year. Ternium’s filings give the Luxembourg-based steel group an effective 89.25% stake in its Mexican business, including shares held through Ternium Argentina. That puts 2.32 million tonnes in Luxembourg on the owners’ map. We leave the remaining 0.28 million unassigned. 3

At Ras al-Khair in Saudi Arabia, all three shareholders of a planned steel plate complex are known. China’s Baosteel holds half. Saudi Aramco and Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund each hold a quarter. The fund owns real equity. Its quarter is grey on the industrial-owner map because the fund is a financial investor. The project stays on the tracker map while its construction stage is under review. 4

How much steel does a new furnace add?

At Port Talbot in South Wales, the map points back to India. Tata Steel is building an electric furnace at the British works. The new furnace is rated at 3.2 million tonnes a year. The old works had capacity for about five million. When the electric furnace opens, steelmaking can resume at Port Talbot, with less capacity than before. On the map of additional tonnes, its contribution is zero. 5

China’s largest construction total also includes furnaces built to replace others. In Hubei, the plan for a new steelworks in Huangshi assigns it 2.25 million tonnes a year from six furnaces to be withdrawn at three plants. Huangshi gains a mill while permitted capacity across the linked sites stays flat or falls. Sweden shows how small the gain can be even when a replacement is larger. At Luleå, SSAB puts the new mill at 2.5 million tonnes against 2.3 million today. Only the difference, 200,000 tonnes, belongs in the additions column. 6 7

We documented 25.3 million tonnes of positive additions at 10 plants in 5 countries. Companies based in the host country account for 12.25 million tonnes of that total, overseas companies for 8.47 million, and 4.58 million remain unassigned. India alone accounts for 17.05 million of the checked additions. Across the tracker, 28 sites contribute zero to the checked additions and 69 still have an unresolved net amount.

How much does the ownership result depend on those old furnaces? Apply only the capacity corrections we could measure and leave every unresolved site at its full tracker rating. The share traced to domestic owners falls from 51.5% to 46.5%. Domestic companies own a narrow majority of the measured capacity on the construction list. We cannot yet say they own a majority of the steelmaking capacity the world will gain. 8
