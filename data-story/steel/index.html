Who owns the next steel boom?

India is building the mills. Where are the companies behind them based?

At Hazira, Gujarat, three new converters are designed to give a steelworks on the Tapi estuary room to make another six million tonnes of crude steel a year. The company building them is ArcelorMittal Nippon Steel India. Follow its ownership through a holding company in Luxembourg and two steelmakers come into view. ArcelorMittal, headquartered in Luxembourg, holds 60%. Nippon Steel, headquartered in Japan, holds 40%. 1

All six million tonnes belong in Gujarat when the question is where the steel can be made. Divide the same capacity according to the sponsors’ equity and 3.6 million tonnes a year trace to Luxembourg and 2.4 million to Japan. The furnaces stay where they are. What changes is whose stake in them we can see.

How often does the industrial map of India change like this when we look up the owners? Global Energy Monitor’s September 2026 release lists 37.55 million tonnes of annual crude-steel capacity under construction across 13 Indian sites. It records more than eight times as much capacity in announcements. A board can approve a mill years before the first converter goes up, so we started with the construction list. Its recorded parent companies trace 22.23 million tonnes a year to India, 14.20 million overseas and 1.12 million to unresolved stakes or headquarters. Would the Indian lead survive a check on what was actually being built? We began with the largest projects. 2

At Dolvi, down the west coast from Hazira, JSW Steel photographed a converter going into place. Its expansion will lift the Maharashtra works from 10 million tonnes a year to 15 million. The five-million-tonne addition traces to JSW Steel in Mumbai. Across the country in Odisha, JSW is building the first phase of its Utkal works through a wholly owned company. GEM counts seven million tonnes of converter capacity there. JSW puts the first phase of the plant at five million. Five million is how much steel the first phase is expected to add at Utkal, so it earns the same weight on the map as Dolvi. Together they account for ten million tonnes a year. 3

At Ghugus, another Maharashtra project shows how easy it is to count the same metal twice. Lloyds Metals and Energy is adding a steel melting shop to a site that already makes iron. Its clearance gives the new rolling mill a capacity of 1.2 million tonnes a year. The furnaces that make the steel feeding it are rated at 1.05 million. Steel passes through both stages. The melting shop measures how much new crude steel the site can make, all of it attributable to the Mumbai-based operator. 4

Across these four projects, Indian steelmakers account for 11.05 of the 17.05 million tonnes of annual capacity, about 65%. The rest belongs to Hazira’s two sponsors. All four ownership chains can be assigned without estimating a missing stake. But the four additions cover less than half the capacity in the tracker’s construction list. To see why that matters, look at the largest site we set aside. 5

In March, the same partners who own Hazira laid a foundation stone at Anakapalle in Andhra Pradesh. The project had environmental clearance for 8.2 million tonnes a year. In July, ArcelorMittal still described it as announced. One outstanding condition was a long-term supply agreement for iron ore. Its September-quarter report used the same description. GEM had already placed the 8.2 million tonnes in construction. It is a consequential difference on a map of who owns what is being built. 6

Count Anakapalle alongside those four projects and overseas steelmakers account for 14.2 of 25.25 million tonnes a year, or 56%. Ownership can swing back again. At Neelachal in Odisha, Tata’s September-quarter report describes roads being graded and equipment being ordered. If we also accept GEM’s 3.49 million tonnes under construction there, India-based sponsors edge ahead again, with 50.6%. 5

At Ludhiana, the construction list needs a correction in the other direction. GEM still carried its furnace as under construction in September. Tata Steel had inaugurated it in March and reported that production was ramping up in July. Its 750,000-tonne-a-year furnace was already operating. 7

Tata’s map extends beyond India. Its British business broke ground on an electric arc furnace at Port Talbot in South Wales in July 2025, after the old blast furnaces closed. Tata Steel announced a £750 million investment, alongside a UK government grant of up to £500 million. An Indian steelmaker can back a mill in Britain just as a Japanese steelmaker can back one in Gujarat. The two contributions describe how the Welsh furnace is being paid for. Only the ownership records tell us who owns the business. 8

Hazira answers the ownership question cleanly for one mill. For the four Indian expansions where the project scope, construction evidence and sponsor chain line up, Indian steelmakers back the larger share. A verdict on India’s whole buildout turns on something more prosaic than the nationality in a company name. It turns on which furnaces have actually started going up, and how much more steel their sites will be able to make.
